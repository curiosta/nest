Start a rural tech lab in your village

A practical guide for NGOs, schools and Gram Panchayats that want a hands-on, multi-skill lab based on the Vigyan Ashram model: the DBRT diploma for youth, or the IBT subject in schools. It covers routes, space, tools, safety, instructors, funding, honest economics and compliance. Every figure links to its source and shows its year. Where we could not confirm something, it carries a To confirm badge with the reason.

About this guide

Vigyan Ashram, at Pabal in Pune district, was started in 1983 by Dr S. S. Kalbag. Its approach, "Rural Development through Education System", rests on four principles: learning while doing, multi-skill training, the school as a production centre (students provide services and the community pays for them), and the instructor as an entrepreneur with demonstrable skills[2][6].

NEST is independent. This guide describes the Vigyan Ashram model from public documents. It is not written, reviewed or endorsed by Vigyan Ashram or STARS Forum. For current fees, costs, syllabus and training dates, contact Vigyan Ashram or STARS Forum directly: see their sites[5][15].

To confirm means we found the point in a search summary, a secondary copy or an old document, and could not check it on the official page. The short reason follows the badge. Check it before you rely on it.

Safety and the law come first

Read this before any student does paid work

  • Child and adolescent labour. No child (under 14, or the age set by the Right to Education Act) may be employed or permitted to work in any occupation, except helping the family or a family enterprise after school or in vacations, or as an artist. No adolescent (14 to 18) may work in the hazardous occupations and processes in the Schedule: mines, inflammable substances or explosives, and "hazardous processes" as defined in the Factories Act[26]. A 2017 notification expanded the Schedule[27]. The amended Act has no stated exemption for school training. To confirmHow the Act treats paid "production centre" work by students in training is a legal question we cannot settle.
  • Electrical work. Under the CEA safety regulations 2023 (regulation 31), electrical installation work must be done by a State-licensed electrical contractor, supervised by a person with a certificate of competency and carried out by a person holding a State permit, with narrow exceptions such as replacing lamps, fans, fuses, switches and small appliances[28]. Regulation 31(2) adds that work done in breach of this must not be energised or connected to the supply (checked in the gazette text on 12 Oct 2026). Paid house wiring for villagers must go through a licensed contractor.
  • Selling food. Food businesses with turnover up to Rs 1.5 crore need FSSAI registration (thresholds in force from 1 Apr 2026)[29]. Register before the lab sells pickles, flour, snacks or any processed food.
  • Child protection. Odisha's 2025 contract for school vocational training partners requires a child-protection policy and POCSO/POSH committees[30]. Adopt the same, even if you never bid for such a contract.

Get a labour lawyer's written opinion before students do any paid service work, and before anyone under 18 uses welding, grinding, flammable gas or mains electricity outside supervised practice.

Tips for this step

  • No paid work for under-14s. Keep paid service orders for adults and older trainees, and keep under-14s to learning tasks. NEST suggestion
  • Write a one-page risk check for each activity (welding, wiring, grinding, food processing, animal handling) before the first session, and post it at the station. NEST suggestion
  • Basics on the wall: first-aid kit and a trained first-aider, a fire extinguisher near welding, eye protection for grinding and welding, RCCB on the lab supply. NEST suggestion

DBRT or IBT: two separate routes

These are two different programmes, not one programme delivered through the other. Pick one, or run both with different learners.

DBRT: a diploma for youth

The Diploma in Basic Rural Technology is certified by NIOS (National Institute of Open Schooling)[1][3]. NIOS lists 8th pass as the entry level, 600 hours, and four papers (codes 452 to 455): Our Health, Agriculture & Animal Husbandry, Rural Engineering, and Our Home Environment, each marked 30 theory + 50 practical + 20 internal[3]. At Pabal it is a one-year residential course for ages 16 to 22, followed by an apprenticeship year[1]. The 2024-25 batch had 61 students[10].

IBT: a subject inside a school

Introduction to Basic Technology is a pre-vocational subject for Classes 8 to 10, taught one day a week (about 10 periods), started in 3 schools in 1987[6][13]. It is recognised under NSQF as the Multi-Skill Foundation Course (MSFC), Levels 1 and 2[4][7], and the Maharashtra SSC board examines it in Class 10[6]. CBSE also offers a Multi Skill Foundation Course (code 416)[20].

Four areas, both routes. In the Vigyan Ashram model, training covers Engineering (fabrication, construction, carpentry, drawing, costing), Energy & Environment (electrical work, motor rewinding, survey, solar, biogas), Home & Health (sewing, food processing, a rural lab), and Agriculture & Animal Husbandry (polyhouse, poultry, goat, dairy, nursery). Computers, design tools and AI tools are now part of DBRT[1].

Three ways a village NGO can start

  1. IBT in a partner school. The school signs up for at least 3 years, sets up the lab and selects instructors to Vigyan Ashram's guidelines; Vigyan Ashram trains and monitors; the board examines Class 10[4]. This needs a board that recognises IBT/MSFC, or a CBSE school.
  2. A DBRT-style course for youth. To award the NIOS diploma you must be, or partner with, a NIOS Accredited Vocational Institution (AVI). For NGOs this includes registration as a society or trust and at least 3 years of existence[25]. To confirmThe NIOS site timed out for us, so we could not read the current AVI norms or fees. Without accreditation, run an uncertified course, or send learners to an existing DBRT centre (see Who runs it today).
  3. A community lab without a diploma. Run multi-skill sessions and service work, and have learners take an RPL or PMKVY assessment for an NSQF certificate (see Funding routes). NEST suggestion

Tips for this step

  • Match the route to your learners. School-age children: IBT through the school. Out-of-school youth aged 16 and over: a DBRT-style course. Adults with some skills: the community lab with RPL. NEST suggestion
  • Plan for three years, not one month. IBT is a minimum three-year commitment in the Vigyan Ashram model[4]. A short taster workshop is fine, but budget beyond it. NEST suggestion

Plan and partners

  • School and Panchayat. For IBT, the school provides the rooms, school time, instructors from the community and one maths or science teacher to teach theory and coordinate[4].
  • A lab committee. The IBT guidelines ask each school to set up a committee: the headmaster, sarpanch, SHG chairperson, an advocate, a press reporter and a community entrepreneur[4].
  • Training and syllabus. In the Vigyan Ashram model, Vigyan Ashram provides technical support, instructor training, content, and mentoring and monitoring for the life of the project, while the school runs the programme day to day, buys raw material and pays instructors[6]. STARS Forum (Skills Training for Advancement in Rural Societies), founded in 2010 at Pabal, is a network of NGOs and individuals working on rural vocational skills[15].

Tips for this step

  • Hold one joint meeting first with the NGO board, the Gram Panchayat, the school and interested youth and parents, and write down who provides what. NEST suggestion
  • Ask Vigyan Ashram or STARS Forum for current terms before you budget. The only per-school figures we found are from 2016. NEST suggestion

Space, tools and safety by area

Space and utilities. For IBT, Vigyan Ashram asks the school for two classrooms (800 to 1,000 sq ft), a quarter acre of farm land (can be rented), an electricity connection and a water connection[4].

The NSQF MSFC Level 1 curriculum (Govt of Maharashtra, 2015) gives a "suggestive" tool list and says only basic tools should be bought so that routine tasks can be practised[4]. A short starter set from that list:

Energy & environmentMultimeter and clamp meter, wire strippers and crimpers, MCB/RCCB, earthing set, non-contact voltage detector, rubber matting, inverter and battery, solar lights; models of a biogas plant and soak pit[4]Training boards, not live house wiring; licensed contractor for paid work[28]
Agriculture & animal husbandryHoes, spades, pruners, grafting and budding knives, soil testing kit, soil auger, shade net, polybags, watering cans, wheelbarrow, weighing balance[4]Protective clothing: apron, mask, gloves, boots (on the MSFC list)[4]; safe storage of any chemicals

The published list mixes in items from other trades (refrigeration gear and a foreign electrical code), so trim it to what your services need.

Tips for this step

  • Keep a tool register and an issue log. Vigyan Ashram lists pilferage of tools as an implementation challenge[4]. The skill kit has a template. NEST suggestion
  • Buy for your first service menu, not the whole list: start with the services your village will actually pay for. NEST suggestion

Instructors: local artisans, with care

  • The model. Instructors should be village entrepreneurs with demonstrable skills; local skilled youth are trained as instructors, and a science or maths teacher handles theory[6]. An instructor can run their own business from the school workshop with students helping[13].
  • The paper bar. The NSQF MSFC curriculum sets the instructor qualification as an ITI certificate, diploma or degree in a relevant field, HSC-vocational, DBRT, or RPL Level 3 or above[4]. An artisan without certificates can qualify through RPL (Recognition of Prior Learning).
  • Training. In 2016 Vigyan Ashram's support covered two 6-day programmes at Pabal for 4 instructors per school; instructors without basic technical skills needed a further 1-month course at extra cost[6].
  • Known problems. Building instructor confidence takes time, and instructors are hard to keep if they are not paid the honorarium they need[4]. As Lend-A-Hand India scaled its version, community instructors were replaced by staff with vocational certificates[20].
  • Government-funded school courses work differently. Odisha's 2025 contract needs degree- or diploma-qualified vocational trainers aged 18 to 45, budgeted at Rs 20,000 a month each[30]. Artisans fit there as guest lecturers.

Tips for this step

  • Four instructors, at least one a woman. That is what the IBT guidelines ask for[4].
  • Pair each artisan with a qualified coordinator, pay a fixed honorarium plus a share of service income, and enrol them for RPL so they hold a certificate. NEST suggestion

Honest economics

The "school as a production centre" idea is real: students install drip irrigation, rent out knapsack sprayers, make tree guards and poultry cages, build soak pits and test well water[4]. But the published figures do not show service income covering the running costs. These are the only figures we found; each is the source's own, with its year.

Tips for this step

  • Treat service income as a learning tool and a partial offset, not the business model. Fund at least three years from grants. NEST suggestion
  • Keep a price list and an order log from day one, so you can report real income against real costs. NEST suggestion

Realistic funding routes

Beware of "NABARD grants for NGOs" posts. NABARD has publicly warned that posts advertising grants for NGOs under its Skill Development & Entrepreneurship among Rural Youth (SDERY) scheme are misleading and that it issued no such announcement. Use only nabard.org or your NABARD Regional Office[34]. Never pay anyone to "process" a grant.

Registrations and compliance

  • 12A and 80G have new names. From 1 Apr 2026 the Income-tax Act 2025 applies: registration as a Registered Non-Profit Organisation under section 332 (the old 12A/12AB) and approval under section 354 so donors can claim a deduction (the old 80G). Both use Form No. 105, and orders come in Form No. 107. Regular registration lasts 5 tax years, or 10 if income was at most Rs 5 crore in each of the two previous years[42]. Registrations and approvals granted under the 1961 Act stay valid under the new Act unless they conflict with it (section 536(2)(j)); apply in Form No. 105 at least six months before yours expires[43].
  • FCRA (foreign money). Registration is open to associations operating for at least 3 years; newer ones can seek prior permission for a defined project. All foreign contributions arrive first in the FCRA account at SBI New Delhi Main Branch; at most 20% may go on administration; annual return FC-4; registration lasts 5 years. The 2026 rules print purposes and states on the certificate and require at least Rs 10 lakh of foreign contribution used over two years for renewal[44]. Registration also asks for Rs 15 lakh spent on core activities over three years[45]. To confirmThat threshold is from the 2019 FAQ and may have changed.
  • Labour, electrical and food rules: see Safety and the law.

Launch checklist and KPIs

  1. Pick the route (DBRT or IBT) and the learners.
  2. Legal shell: trust, society or Section 8 company; Income-tax registration (Registrations).
  3. Partners and committee: school, Panchayat, a lab committee[4]; ask Vigyan Ashram or STARS Forum about training.
  4. Asset map: rooms, land, power, water[4], and the artisans who could teach.
  5. Three-year budget from real quotes.
  6. Instructor plan: four instructors including a woman[4], RPL, honorarium.
  7. Safety and safeguarding: risk checks, PPE, licensed contractor for paid wiring, FSSAI before selling food, child-protection policy, a labour lawyer's opinion (Safety).
  8. First service menu with prices and an order log.
  9. Measure from day one (below).

What to measure

  • From Samagra Shiksha's vocational KPIs: enrolment (including girls and children with special needs), retention and drop-out, fully equipped labs, practical-to-theory hours, field visits, students passing with a vocational subject, progression to ITI, polytechnic or B.Voc, and placement or apprenticeship[35].
  • From the Vigyan Ashram model: number and value of community services, Class 10 results, and the share going on to technical courses[6][8].
  • Also track: instructor retention, service income against running cost, safety incidents (aim for zero), and enterprises started by alumni within a year. NEST suggestion

Who runs it today

Most outcome figures below come from the organisations themselves or from studies they commissioned. The 2022 Springer chapter relies on self-reported surveys and calls for a controlled long-term study[20].

Vigyan Ashram (Pabal)

IBT in "140+ schools in Maharashtra benefiting over 15,000 students" (its 2022-23 report)[7]; 165 IBT instructors and 50 DBRT students supported in 2023-24[12].

Lend-A-Hand India

Took IBT to more schools from 2005 and developed it into the MSFC; adopted by more than 600 secondary schools; 331 Maharashtra schools with 18,210 students. Reported results include 56% of MSFC students going on to further vocational education, against 20% of others[20].

UP "Learning by Doing"

UNICEF, Samagra Shiksha UP, Vigyan Ashram and STARS Forum: from 60 schools in 2021 to labs in 1,772 schools plus 570 PM SHRI schools in 2024-25, with 2,160 teachers trained (their own report)[19][17].

STARS Forum

Runs IBT in 15 Maharashtra schools[16] and reports helping spread school vocational training from Maharashtra to states such as Uttar Pradesh[18].

Other DBRT centres

Loksadhana runs IBT and a one-year residential DBRT[21]; Govardhan Skill Centre (Wada, Palghar) runs a one-year DBRT[22]; Narmadalaya's VIRT (Madhya Pradesh) runs a two-year DBRT[23].

Schools as development centres

BAIF used Ashram schools in Nandurbar as demonstration centres, with expert farmers as demonstrators, in 48 schools from 2009[24]. An independent overview of IBT is in Azim Premji University's Learning Curve[14].
