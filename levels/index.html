How roles are evolving

The AI role ladder

Traditional job roles are evolving with AI. NEST uses a simple hierarchy of four roles, L1 to L4, to show how: each rung builds on the one before, from using expert-written AI skills on the job to building the tools those skills run on. It applies to both Work with AI and Work for AI.

The four rungs

  1. L1Intelligent ProxyUses expert AI skills; does the physical work
  2. L2OrchestratorRuns several AI tools and a crew's workflow
  3. L3Skill developerWrites the skills others use
  4. L4Harness and model builderBuilds the tools skills run on

No rung is a job title or a pay grade, and we give no salaries.

  1. L1 Intelligent Proxy

    Uses expert-written AI skills on the job and does the physical work: the most accessible rung today.

    An Intelligent Proxy works with a second brain of expert skills. The second brain is the AI plus the expert-designed skills the person carries with them; the person using it is the Intelligent Proxy.

    What you do: Uses the AI tools at hand plus skills written by experts, and does the physical part on site.

    Real examples:

    • Farmers ask Kisan e-Mitra, a voice AI chatbot, in their own language
    • Non-specialist health workers screen patients with AI diagnostic aids
    • Urban Company professionals follow an app that guides diagnosis and SOPs

    Skills to learn: Clear instructions to an agent; checking an answer against what you see; when to stop and ask a qualified person.

    Physical-work example: A rural lab instructor loads the rural tech lab skill to plan a safe workshop session, then runs it with the students.

  2. L2 Orchestrator

    Runs several AI tools and a crew's workflow, and checks what the agents produce.

    What you do: Runs several AI agents on one job and reviews what each one produces before anything is used.

    Real examples:

    • Adani Green's operation centre watches sites across states and suggests corrective action
    • Securitas operators verify alarms before anyone is dispatched
    • Mahindra Logistics' LogiOne puts warehouses and transporters in one view

    Skills to learn: Splitting a job into tasks; giving each agent the right skill and files; reviewing and correcting output; keeping a record.

    Physical-work example: A fair organiser has one agent draft employer invites, one build the floor plan and one check the safety plan, then reviews all three before the fair.

  3. L3 Skill developer

    Turns hard-won experience into tested skills that others load.

    What you do: Designs and tests skills that let other people's agents do a job the way an expert would.

    Real examples:

    • Cult Training Academy's certification course for new trainers
    • DGT's Craft Instructor Training Scheme for ITI instructors
    • Garuda Aerospace's drone-pilot course, from theory to simulation to flying

    Skills to learn: Writing step-by-step instructions, checks and stop-and-ask rules; testing a skill on real cases; versioning and licensing it.

    Physical-work example: A senior electrician writes and tests a fault-finding skill that junior technicians' agents follow on site.

  4. L4 Harness and model builder

    Builds and tests the models and tools that skills run on, with the people who use them.

    What you do: A domain expert, or an engineer working with one, who builds the AI tools, harnesses, evaluations (evals) or models for their own domain: crop-disease models, lab imaging AI, robot controllers, grid digital twins, data-centre cooling optimisation.

    Real examples:

    • DeepMind's machine-learning cooling for Google data centres
    • Addverb's own robot software
    • ITC's Krishi Mitra GenAI co-pilot

    Skills to learn: Programming; how agents call tools; designing evals that measure whether an agent did the job right; how models are trained.

    Physical-work example: A team builds an eval that checks whether an agent's job-fair safety plan covers every rule in the organiser skill.

Expert-designed skills to start from, at any rung: the job-fair organiser skill kit and the rural tech lab skill kit. Read and use them at L1, combine them at L2, use them as models at L3, and test agents against them at L4.

L1 in practice: the Intelligent Proxy

The first rung comes from NEST Collective (Manoj Sahukar): when an expert designs a SKILL, a written set of steps, checks and safety rules that an AI agent follows, every agent that loads it can work like an expert. The person directing that agent does not have to be the expert. They are an Intelligent Proxy: they bring the place, the hands, the judgement and the responsibility, and the skill brings the know-how. NEST is about work that needs a person in a real place, so the proxy is that person, directing agents through skills, a phone browser or the command line while doing the part only a human on the spot can do.

  • A field technician on a site asks an agent on a phone browser to walk through a fault-finding checklist written by an experienced engineer, then does the physical repair.
  • A farmer asks an agent to read a scheme page, fill the form in the browser and list the papers to carry, then goes to the office with them.
  • A fair organiser loads NEST's job-fair skill and has the agent draft the timeline, employer invites and safety plan, then runs the day on the ground.
  • Vibe coding: someone who is not a programmer asks an agent to do anything that can be done with a command line or a web browser (rename files, build a sheet, make a small web page) and checks the result.

Work for AI: recording skills for robots

Startups in India and abroad now pay workers to record their tasks on first-person (egocentric) cameras, so robots and AI models can learn how a skilled person moves. It is a Work for AI job, and it has its own ladder.

  1. L1 Skilled worker recorded as data

    Does their normal task while wearing a head, chest or wrist camera (sometimes motion sensors or gloves), so a robot can learn from their hands and choices. Some work is teleoperation: steering a robot through the task. NEST view

    Real examples:

    • Workers at a Karur textile factory wore head cameras and smart glasses supplied by Objectways
    • Egolab.AI collects first-person footage from garment workers in Delhi NCR
    • Scroll: Egolab's head-mounted cameras were worn by garment workers at a Pearl Global unit in Gurugram; its company document speaks of “100 million hours of egocentric footage”
    • Micro1 contract workers, including in India, record household chores with head-mounted phones

  2. L2 Annotator and quality reviewer

    Labels actions in the footage (hand poses, grasp points, start and end of each step) and accepts or rejects clips against the brief. NEST view

    Real examples:

    • Annotators label hand poses, object locations and grasp points frame by frame
    • Micro1 videos are reviewed by AI and a human, then annotated by a human team
    • OkayGo runs consent checks, metadata tagging and multi-layer QC

  3. L3 Task and capture designer

    Writes the task lists, capture protocols and checklists that tell recorders what to do and how, often a senior worker from the trade. NEST view

    Real examples:

    • EgoData uses standardised capture protocols and task checklists
    • Graveiens AI trains participants on each client's capture protocol

  4. L4 Robot and foundation-model trainer

    Trains or fine-tunes robot and vision-language-action models on these recordings and tests them on robots. NEST view

    Real examples:

    • Human Archive is developing ways to fine-tune models on its data and test them on robots
    • Open X-Embodiment pools robot datasets to train generalist RT-X policies

Published datasets these roles feed:

  • Ego4D: large egocentric dataset of daily-life activity, filmed in many countries
  • Ego-Exo4D: first- and third-person video of skilled activities such as cooking and bike repair
  • Egocentric-10K: an egocentric dataset collected only in real factories

If you are asked to record your work

NEST guidance, not legal advice. NEST view

  • Consent in writing. Know what is recorded (video, audio, motion), who will use it and for what, and how to stop. Bystanders and family members should be asked too.
  • Fair pay, agreed before you start. Ask how time is counted, including set-up and rejected clips. Never pay a fee to join.
  • Data ownership and licence. Ask who owns the recordings, whether they can be resold, and whether your face and voice are removed.
  • Not a surveillance tool. Recordings for robot training should not be used to score or discipline you; ask for this in the agreement.
  • Know the trade-off. These recordings help machines learn your task. Use the work to move up the ladder: reviewing, designing tasks, or maintaining the robots.

News coverage of consent and surveillance concerns: Indian Express explainer, Inc42, Scroll (workers say consent was not obtained; Egolab says it took consent “in our own way”), MIT Technology Review (1 Apr 2026).
